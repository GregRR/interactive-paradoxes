5. Benford's Law (The First-Digit Anomaly)

In naturally occurring datasets, the number 1 appears as the leading digit ~30% of the time. Human intuition expects an even ~11.1% spread.

7. Simpson's Paradox

Below are two groups of students, each showing a positive relationship between study hours and test scores — more studying, better scores, within each group. But watch what happens when the two groups are combined into one dataset: the overall trend reverses. Nothing about the underlying data changes; only how it's grouped does. This is an illustrative example built to demonstrate the effect clearly — drag the slider slowly from left to right to watch the story unfold.

Separate groups Group trends Combined Reversed trend

This illustrative scenario is a standard textbook way of demonstrating Simpson's Paradox with continuous data. The same reversal shows up in real studies too — for example, in a 1986 British Medical Journal comparison of kidney stone treatments, Treatment A outperformed Treatment B on both small stones (93% vs. 87% success) and large stones (73% vs. 69% success), yet Treatment B appeared to win overall (83% vs. 78%) once the case mix was ignored. Further reading: Simpson's Paradox on Wikipedia.

8. The Prosecutor's Fallacy (Base-Rate Neglect)

A test can be highly accurate and still be wrong most of the time it flags someone — if what it's looking for is rare enough. This is one of the most common reasoning errors in medicine and the courtroom: people hear "the test is 90% accurate" and assume a positive result means a 90% chance of being right. It usually doesn't. What matters just as much as the test's accuracy is how rare the thing it's testing for was to begin with — the base rate.
